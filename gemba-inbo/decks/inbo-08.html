Criteria-based screening · the 3M RWW method

Three questions before you commit: Real, Win, Worth it.

01

Real

  • Is there a real market you can serve with this product or service?
  • Market size, potential pricing, availability of technology
  • Can you produce and deliver it at high volume?
02

Win

  • Can you win with this opportunity?
  • Can you establish a sustainable competitive advantage?
  • Can you patent or brand the idea?
  • Are you more capable of executing it than competitors?
03

Worth it

  • Is the opportunity worth it financially?
  • Do you have the resources needed?
  • Will the investment earn appropriate returns?

Five habits

Business Model Canvas tips.

26
Tip 1 of 5

Include enough detail to see why an alternative loses.

A one-word sticky doesn’t explain anything. Write enough that a reader understands why this choice beats the next best option.

Example: Apple iPod/iPhone / iTunes — “seamless music experience,” not just “music.” What additional, better features does an alternative like Spotify give?
27
Tip 2 of 5

Too many stickies bury the model in noise.

A canvas covered edge-to-edge in notes is unreadable. Keep each block to the handful of assumptions that actually drive the decision.

Rule of thumb: if you can’t read it from across the room, cut it.
28
Tip 3 of 5

If removing one piece doesn’t break the model, cut it.

Test every sticky: take it off the canvas. If the business model still stands, it wasn’t essential — it was decoration.

Generic back-office items — “HR,” “project management” — rarely survive this test.
29
Tip 4 of 5

Color-code by customer segment or value proposition.

When one canvas serves several segments, color lets you trace a single segment’s path through every block at a glance.

Watch for overlapping stickies and orphan elements — both are signals something’s unresolved.
30
Tip 5 of 5

A value proposition names the problem solved — not a feature list.

“Product,” “Feature 1,” “Feature 2” is a spec sheet. A value proposition says what job gets done and why that matters.

Use the canvas to articulate the model

What makes your start-up work? Value creation analysis, next.

  1. State the thesis. Carry over your X·Y·Z from a prior session.
  2. Use the BMC to articulate the business model. What are the key sectoral assumptions?
  3. Identify points of comparison. Alternative business models, the customer's next-best option, closest operational analogues, other industries.
  4. Map the differences between your business model and each point of comparison.
  5. Demand-side analysis. Why will customers choose you over the alternatives? What are their risks?
  6. Profitability and risk analysis. Can you deliver profitably? How does the model manage the customer's risk?

Value Creation Analysis · step 3 · worked example: Better Place & NIO

Choose comparisons that make the differences visible.

01

Alternative solution

  • A standard gasoline car, refueled at any of ~150,000 U.S. gas stations — the incumbent product and its infrastructure, both already built.
02

Alternative ownership model

  • Tesla — you own the battery outright and charge or fast-charge it yourself, with all the repair and depreciation risk that comes with owning a $20K+ pack.
03

Similar technology need

  • Smartphone battery charging — the same problem of frequent charge cycles, degradation, and who bears the replacement cost, solved without swapping.
04

Competitive backdrop

  • BYD and China’s price war — the next-best option for many customers is simply a cheaper, well-built standard EV, not a subscription battery model at all.
Same method as the earlier Soko example: pick comparisons, then map the differences.

Group formation

Teams re-form mid-course — a small taste of startup turnover.

  1. Individual, then team. The course starts with individual work; you reorganize into teams partway through.
  2. Idea survives → recruit. You gain the right, and the responsibility, to build out your team.
  3. Idea doesn’t survive → join. Pair with a surviving project; from here, the unit of work is the group.
  4. Why it matters. Recruiting after resources are granted, and becoming useful on someone else’s team fast, are core founder skills — new ventures have high turnover.
